Edward — Faculty Debrief

Final diagnosis: Superior vena cava syndrome. This case emphasizes venous obstruction in the setting of malignancy and thrombosis risk.

Reasoning framework

  1. Generate a broad doorway differential.
  2. Use active history and targeted physical examination to seek discriminating findings.
  3. Consider disease prevalence and pretest probability.
  4. Build and compare illness scripts.
  5. Choose tests that meaningfully update probability.
  6. Use diagnostic guardrails to identify cognitive bias and predictable misses.
  7. Reflect on the process rather than treating one case as a pass/fail test.

Faculty doorway differential

SVC obstruction from tumor, SVC thrombosis, cellulitis/port infection, radiation dermatitis, drug reaction including morbilliform eruption/DRESS, and irritant dermatitis are useful starting considerations.

Discriminating features

Important targeted features include arm/neck/face swelling, dyspnea or cough, plethora, jugular venous distention, chest-wall collateral veins, port findings, radiation distribution, medication exposure, and prior venous thrombosis.

Know Your Test

Contrast-enhanced CT is generally the key imaging study for suspected SVC obstruction; angiographic evaluation may occasionally be needed depending on management.

Diagnostic guardrails

A useful reflection in this case is focalism: anticoagulation may feel reassuring and cause the clinician to discount thrombosis or venous obstruction despite malignancy, an upper-extremity DVT, a central venous port, and the examination pattern.

Take-home point

The goal is not to get every case right. The goal is to use a repeatable process that gives you a reasonable opportunity to identify important diagnoses and reduces predictable diagnostic error over time.
